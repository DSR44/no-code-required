The people who build frontier AI spent September telling anyone who'd listen that maybe, possibly, we should slow down. Dario Amodei published a plan to "pace the frontier." Sam Altman echoed it. Even Elon Musk nodded along. Meanwhile, two companies with rockets and warehouses decided the debate was someone else's problem — and kept pouring concrete and welding satellites. If you build anything with AI tools, this gap between what the labs say and what the industry does is the most useful signal you'll get all year.

And the gap is real. This isn't a story about the same handful of chatbots getting shinier — it connects straight to the tools you already touch, from [AI-generated product images showing up on Amazon](/posts/amazon-ai-generated-product-images/) to the model you pick for your next workflow.

## What "pace the frontier" actually says

On September 12, the Anthropic CEO laid out three proposals: independent third-party evaluators embedded inside frontier labs to monitor safety practices and incidents, coordination on safety standards among major AI companies in democratic countries, and some form of international coordination.

Read that carefully and you'll notice what's missing: nothing in it says "slower." As TechCrunch's Anthony Ha pointed out on the Equity podcast, these are monitoring and coordination measures. They could result in things slowing down, but nothing in them requires it. The word the CEOs prefer is "pace," not "slowdown" — and the difference matters. Kirsten Korosec's take on the same episode was blunter: consensus can be a good thing, and it can also be the beginnings of a cartel.

The details are thin enough that even friendly coverage struggled. What would the evaluators actually measure? What triggers a pause? Nobody's said. When the September 19 backlash cycle crested, Jensen Huang ended up on stage at the All-In Summit taking a call from President Trump and saying the quiet part loudly: an AI slowdown is not going to happen. When the man selling every GPU in the industry tells you the buildout continues, believe him — he has better data than the bloggers.

## What Amazon and SpaceX did next

While the labs wrote blog posts, the physical buildout accelerated.

SpaceX is reportedly confident it will put NVIDIA's AI chips into orbit by 2027 — the first step toward satellite-based data centers that run AI compute in space. It sounds absurd, and plenty of engineers think the thermodynamics are brutal. That's not the point. The point is what the attempt signals: when terrestrial power grids, water supplies, and zoning boards become the bottleneck, the answer isn't to pace anything. It's to leave the planet.

Amazon, meanwhile, keeps expanding its data center footprint — including new capacity in Eastern Oregon, where it's now spending hundreds of millions of dollars on water replenishment projects, partly because its data centers drink the local supply. When a company invests that heavily in the *water infrastructure under its compute*, it's not planning a slowdown.

Here's the pattern that matters: the companies closest to the compute layer — chips, power, launches — are acting like the debate doesn't exist. The companies selling you models are the ones talking about pacing. And the labs' own economics push the same direction; as my earlier breakdown of the [AI landscape after the regulatory crackdown](/posts/ai-landscape-after-regulatory-crackdown-2026/) showed, the players who benefit from unpaced growth are the ones funding the infrastructure.

## Why neither outcome changes your Monday

I've watched a few cycles of "AI is about to slow down" now, and the practical answer for a solo builder hasn't changed once. You don't control the frontier. You control your workflows.

What you actually control is three things:

**Portability.** Build your workflows so you can swap models. The [AI coding price war](/posts/ai-coding-price-war-what-solo-builders-pay/) already showed what happens when providers compete on price — your tools get cheaper and better *because* nobody slowed down. A workflow locked to one vendor inherits that vendor's bad weeks.

**Skills over subscriptions.** The [subscription price war](/posts/ai-subscription-price-war-what-to-pay-for/) only matters if you're renting capabilities you haven't learned to direct. The person who knows how to brief, evaluate, and iterate an AI workflow barely notices which model is on top this month.

**Shipping now.** The most expensive mistake in every platform shift I've watched was waiting for things to "settle." They don't settle. The builders who shipped mediocre things early learned more than the ones waiting for the perfect moment. The [agents-are-becoming-employees shift](/posts/ai-agents-are-becoming-employees/) is happening in production right now, at companies that stopped deliberating.

## The honest read

Could the labs actually slow down? The TechCrunch crew's consensus: these companies aren't structured in a way where that works. Regulation enforcement is weak, enterprise customers won't switch providers over principles, and investment capital absorbs any losses. Sean O'Kane said it plainly — in practice, none of the market forces that would make a slowdown real are actually happening.

So my working assumption, and the one I'd recommend: the slowdown is a press release. The buildout is a purchase order. Watch what gets bought and built, not what gets said at conferences. When the orbital data center launches — or quietly doesn't — you'll know.
