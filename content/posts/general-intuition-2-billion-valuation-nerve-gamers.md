General Intuition just raised $320 million at a $2.3 billion valuation, less than a year after its initial $134M round, bringing total disclosed funding to $454 million. The company trains AI agents on billions of gameplay clips from Medal's 10 million monthly users, using embedded action labels (button presses, timing) rather than video alone. Khosla Ventures led the latest round, with participation from Jeff Bezos, Eric Schmidt, and researchers from Google DeepMind and MIT.

I covered [their first raise](/posts/general-intuition-ai-agents-video-game-data/) last month. At the time, the pitch felt interesting but speculative. Now there's a working robot, a jobs platform, and an API arriving this summer. Here's what actually changed.

## Why are top investors pouring money into a company that learns from Fortnite?

Because the people who backed OpenAI and Anthropic believe the next breakthrough lives in spatial intelligence, and they think gameplay data is the fastest route there. Khosla Ventures led the round; General Catalyst, Jeff Bezos, Eric Schmidt, Nico Rosberg, and researchers from Google DeepMind and MIT joined in.

The investor list matters more than the dollar figure. These aren't gaming VCs chasing the next battle royale. They're the people who shaped the foundation model era, and they're betting that models which understand physical environments are the next frontier.

Most of the capital goes toward scaling compute through a deal with CoreWeave, focused on pre-training the next version of their model. A smaller portion funds the API they plan to open to outside developers by end of summer.

## How do video game clips turn into a walking robot?

The same model that plays Fortnite also powered a quadrupedal robot around General Intuition's New York office using a single camera. TechCrunch reporters watched the agent play Fortnite for 100 hours straight, then watched it walk a real robot. It bumped into chairs like a toddler and adapted to the new environment after eight minutes of real-world data.

The core idea: a brain that learns to move through a virtual world can transfer that understanding to the physical one. This is what [AI agents becoming employees](/posts/ai-agents-are-becoming-employees/) actually looks like — models that grasp space, time, and cause-and-effect well enough to walk a robot through a room, rather than chatbots drafting emails.

Most competitors try to infer actions from video alone. General Intuition's edge is the action labels baked into Medal's gameplay clips: records of exactly what buttons a player pressed and when. CEO Pim de Witte argues this helps the model distinguish "self" from "environment," which gives it a richer understanding of causality. Watching someone drive versus feeling the steering wheel yourself.

## What is Nerve, and why does it matter for gamers?

Nerve is a jobs marketplace General Intuition recently launched. It lets gamers earn money using their existing hardware, starting with data labeling tasks and eventually moving toward robot teleoperation and higher-skilled work.

The logic tracks. Medal's user base is precisely the generation most exposed to AI-driven displacement, and de Witte wants them to have a stake in what comes next. Instead of extracting training data from gamers and building models that might replace them, Nerve gives those same gamers a way into the AI economy.

It's also a shrewd business move. As the models get more capable, General Intuition will need human oversight for edge cases, safety validation, and real-world data collection. A workforce that already understands spatial environments — because they've spent years inside virtual ones — is a moat that's hard to replicate.

## Is General Intuition drawing ethical lines around military use?

Yes, explicitly. De Witte spent three years in humanitarian work, including time with Doctors Without Borders, and has drawn a firm boundary: no agents built to harm humans. Military applications are prohibited.

This matters because the same technology that walks a robot through an office could walk one through a battlefield. As Silicon Valley grows more comfortable with defense contracts, General Intuition's stance stands out — especially given that investors like Khosla and Bezos have their own positions on defense tech.

The company's chief of staff, Brianna Martin, publicly quit Palantir over its work with ICE. De Witte brought her on specifically because of that decision. It signals the kind of company General Intuition wants to be, and what it refuses to build.

## Can the API justify a $2.3 billion valuation?

The API launch this summer will answer that. It will test whether third-party developers can build useful applications on top of General Intuition's world model, and whether the model holds up across diverse environments rather than one controlled office demo.

The company has a handful of customers in gaming, simulation, and robotics today. But the ecosystem play is what makes the valuation make sense. De Witte wants to be a model provider, not an end-product company. "We're not gonna build a self-driving car company," he said. "We're gonna make it 10 times easier for the next person to build a self-driving car company." Same playbook OpenAI ran with language, applied to spatial intelligence.

If you're building with AI tools — whether it's [automating your business](/posts/build-your-first-automation-in-15-minutes/) or [creating content with AI](/posts/heygen-avatar-v-vs-avatar-3/) — General Intuition's approach suggests the next wave of capable AI agents won't come from scaling language models. They'll come from models that understand the physical world. The API launch will tell us how close that future actually is.

**How much has General Intuition raised in total?**
$454 million in disclosed funding. The company raised $134 million in its initial round, then added $320 million less than a year later at a $2.3 billion valuation. Khosla Ventures led the latest round, with Jeff Bezos, Eric Schmidt, General Catalyst, and researchers from Google DeepMind and MIT participating.

**What data does General Intuition train its AI agents on?**
Billions of gameplay clips from Medal, a gaming platform with 10 million monthly users. The key difference from competitors: Medal's clips include embedded action labels recording exactly which buttons players pressed and when, which helps the model understand causality rather than just watching video.

**What is Nerve?**
Nerve is General Intuition's jobs marketplace for gamers. It lets people earn money with their existing hardware, starting with data labeling tasks and eventually expanding to robot teleoperation and higher-skilled work. It also gives the company a trained workforce for edge-case oversight and safety validation as its models mature.

**Does General Intuition work on military applications?**
No. CEO Pim de Witte, who spent three years in humanitarian work including time with Doctors Without Borders, has prohibited agents built to harm humans and military applications outright. Chief of staff Brianna Martin joined after publicly quitting Palantir over its ICE work.
